Most AI automation savings figures come from one line of maths: how long a task takes, times how often you do it, times what your people cost. That gives you an annual saving.

If an AI automation agency has put a figure like that in front of you, it's probably too big. In this post I'll go through what that sum leaves out, why most businesses can't check it anyway, and the cost almost nobody prices in, which is getting your team and your data ready for AI to work. At the end there's a short list of questions to ask before you sign anything.

## How an AI automation agency gets to its big number

It's very easy for an agency to come in and say: you write this proposal twice a week, it takes 5 hours, five people on your team do it, so here's what you'd save every year. Multiply it out, add a dollar sign, and the decision looks easy.

I understand why they do it. The sum is simple, it's easy to sell, and the owner across the table wants it to be true. The trouble is that the equations behind which automations to go after are quite nuanced, and the simple version skips most of what decides whether an automation pays off.

## What the simple sum leaves out

![The agency estimate multiplies hours per task, times a week, people and hourly rate into an annual saving with nothing taken off. Six things sit under it: how much is automated, human in the loop, the build, running costs, hours versus cash, and the quality of the result.](/blog/ai-automation-agency-savings/what-the-sum-leaves-out.png)

**How much of the task actually gets automated.** The sum assumes the task goes to zero, and it rarely does. Some parts of a process work well with AI and some don't. The share that really comes off someone's plate is usually smaller than the pitch says.

**The human in the loop.** Someone still has to give the AI the right context, check what it produces and fix what's wrong. That time is real, it comes back every time the automation runs, and it's usually underestimated or left out.

**The build.** Agencies rarely count how long it takes to create the automation, especially from the human-in-the-loop side. Your team has to show how the work is done, test what comes back and keep giving feedback until it's right. That's time out of their week before anything is saved.

**What it costs to run.** Tokens, the tools it sits on, and the maintenance when something it connects to changes. Token costs move around a lot depending on the model you use and the pricing at the time. A savings figure with none of this taken off it isn't the saving you'll see.

**Whether the hours become money.** Hours saved get counted as cash saved. If the time goes back into work that wins or keeps customers, great. If it doesn't, the saving only exists in the spreadsheet.

**Whether the result is as good.** This is the one that worries me most. Nobody is tracking the conversion rate on these proposals. The old process might take longer, but the proposals it produces might be more original and better connected to what the customer needs. Sometimes the old version is just a simple email, and it works better. Swap it for something faster that wins less often and you've lost money.

## Most businesses can't check the number anyway

The hardest part is that most businesses don't have clear metrics around any of this. They don't know how long proposals really take, how many get accepted, or what the team's time goes on week to week.

So a lot of our job is setting up that reporting first, so that over 12 months you can clearly see what an automation is doing. Until it's in place, you can't be 100% sure about an automation. You have to trust that the time saved is enough, while still counting everything else above.

## Where the business and the team want different things

There's a second gap that the savings figure never shows, and it's between what the business wants from AI and what the team doing the work wants.

At the top, a business wants highly agentic, automated processes with as little human input as possible. That isn't always how it's put, but deep down it's usually the goal.

The people doing the work see something different. They know AI can't do everything they need perfectly right now. Usually the tools are capable enough. What holds them back is their own setup: the AI isn't connected to the right data, security settings limit which models they can use, or they run into token limits. Within that setup they couldn't do what the business wants even if they tried.

So the way they really use AI is quite ad hoc. It handles parts of a process, and other parts it can't. The business has given everyone AI, but it hasn't given them the connection to tools, context and data that would let them use it properly.

## The cost nobody puts in the proposal

That connection is where the real cost sits. If your team is connected to your tools and data, how well organised is it? Is it AI legible? Can the AI search it cleanly?

These are the challenges businesses are actually facing, and a lot of them have their heads in the sand, because doing it right means quite costly manual work. Someone has to get the information into one place, organised so a model can use it.

Change management is a cost as well. You're asking people to change a way of working they've used for years, often in a new tool. That takes time and attention from the people you're trying to save time for, and if it's handled badly they'll go back to the old way.

## Finding the balance point between the business and the team

I think the two can go hand in hand, if each side does its part.

![Finding the balance point: the team maps their job into tasks to hand over to AI and work to keep (gathering context, original perspective and knowledge, direct client relationships), and the business checks the team has the right models, connected tools and data, security access without friction, and context in one AI-legible place.](/blog/ai-automation-agency-savings/balance-point.png)

**The team decides what to hand over and what to keep.** Ask them to be clear about the parts of the job they don't want to do. Then ask about the parts they believe they'd do best with more time and space. Those are usually the parts that make the AI work better: gathering context, and adding original perspective and knowledge that doesn't exist in an LLM. That's the work you want to free your team up for, because it's your competitive advantage.

**The business is honest about whether it has equipped them.** Before you expect results, ask yourself:

- Do they have access to the right models?
- Does the AI connect to our tools and data properly?
- Do they have the security access they need without friction and blockers?
- Is our context and information stored in one central place that's AI legible?

Get both of those right and you know which work your team wants automated, which work they want to keep, and that they're equipped to do it. As of today, that's the best you can do with AI.

AI will get better. Even so, I think there'll always be an advantage in keeping direct human relationships with your clients, understanding their needs at a real, empathetic level, and putting that understanding into your AI systems. That's how AI gets better at what you do, and it stops you doing AI for the sake of it.

## What to ask before you sign with an AI automation agency

If you've got a savings figure in front of you, these questions will tell you how much of it to trust:

- How much of this task will actually be automated, and what's left for my team?
- How much of my team's time will go into checking and fixing the output?
- How long will the build take, including our time?
- What will it cost to run each month, tokens and maintenance included?
- How will we measure whether it's working, and what are we measuring against today?
- What do my team need (models, access, connected data) for this to work?

An agency that has taken the time to understand your business will have answers. If they can't answer, the figure is a guess.

## Key takeaways

**Why are AI automation savings estimates often too high?** They multiply hours by frequency by wage, and leave out how much really gets automated, the human time to check it, the build, running costs, and whether the saved hours ever become money.

**What hidden costs come with AI automation?** Building and testing it, people checking the output, tokens and maintenance, getting your data organised so AI can use it, and helping the team change how they work.

**How do you measure the ROI of an AI automation?** Set up reporting on the process first, such as time taken and conversion rate, then track it over 12 months. Most businesses don't have that baseline yet.

**How do you get staff to use AI?** Let the team decide which parts of their job to hand over and which to keep, then equip them with the right models, connected tools and data, and security access without friction.

**What should you ask an AI automation agency before signing?** What share of the task will be automated, how much team time it still takes, how long the build will be, what it costs to run, and how you'll measure it.
